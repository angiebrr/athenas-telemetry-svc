Questions the Video track should make you able to answer

Staff-level video questions are rarely definitions. Expect “design it” and “what breaks.” Full list in progress-log.md; these are the ones most likely to come up.

  1. WebRTC vs. HLS for live view: what latency does each give, why, and what does each cost to serve 10,000 viewers? UDP + jitter buffer vs. HTTP segments · CDN cacheability
  2. Why might one camera use WebRTC for live and HLS from KVS for playback? What does the user see at the boundary? live vs. recorded
  3. Where does glass-to-glass latency come from, stage by stage, and which stage would you attack first? latency budget
  4. What does a media server like MistServer do, and why put one between a camera and its viewers? ingest any, output any · fan-out · transmux
  5. What does Kinesis Video Streams handle for you, and what's left to build? How would you pick retention? managed storage · fragments · cost
  6. Transcode vs. transmux: what does each cost per stream, and how do you design a fleet that rarely transcodes? CPU · latency · codec negotiation
  7. Walk through WebRTC setup: signaling, SDP, ICE, STUN, TURN, DTLS, SRTP. What does the standard leave to you? signaling is yours
  8. How does keyframe interval affect join time, bandwidth, and recovery after packet loss? GOP · PLI
  9. getStats() counters are cumulative. Why ship counters rather than rates, and what does that do to your loss tolerance? ties back to M2's at-most-once
  10. A camera's upload is slower than its encoder. Where's the queue, and what drops first? the M2 backpressure question, in a video costume
  11. Design live view and event recording for 1M home cameras: capacity math for ingest, storage, and viewers. system design · back-of-envelope

Why the ring is worth building by hand

Every distributed system is queues joined by networks. The decisions a single in-process queue forces — bounded or unbounded, block or drop, who drains on shutdown — are the same decisions SQS makes at M3, Kafka partitions make at M5, and Redis makes at M6. Building one by hand once means the later ones are recognition rather than discovery.

It also sets a durability boundary. Today an in-memory ring means an accepted request can be lost to a crash — at-most-once delivery. That is exactly the hole the outbox pattern exists to close, which is why M4 comes after M2 rather than instead of it.

Standing questions — TDD and test design

Not milestone-scoped. The vocabulary: level is acceptance / integration / unit (GOOS ch. 1); reusability is separate — a body run against many implementations is a contract test, one written for a single implementation is a unit test. Solitary isolates with doubles, sociable uses real collaborators. A working stand-in is a fake, not a mock.

  1. How many distinct kinds of test are in this repo, and what does each buy that the others can't? taxonomy
  2. The Spec runs at three levels — what does each catch that the one beneath it can't? Which would you delete first if the suite got slow? pyramid · cost per run
  3. Two things in this repo are called “spec” and aren't the same pattern. What's the difference? acceptance spec vs. contract test
  4. Why does specifications live in an exported, non-test package? What does that cost? importability · dependency surface
  5. Seven validation cases downstairs, one invalid-input case in the Spec. Why not all seven at the top? combinatorics go down
  6. Testing against a real in-memory store — mock, stub, or fake? Why does it matter? test double taxonomy
  7. Why outside-in? What goes wrong if you build the store first? design pressure
  8. What does “red for the right reason” mean? Name a time yours wasn't. honest failure
  9. Your acceptance cases need no reset. How, and why beat a reset hook? isolation by construction
  10. A test was awkward to write and you changed the design instead. Walk me through it. listening to the tests

Questions M2 should make you able to answer

A good answer names the trade in both directions, names the failure mode the choice doesn't cover, and says where you'd measure. A definition isn't an answer.

  1. What happens when your consumer is slower than your producer? backpressure vs. load shedding
  2. How did you size that buffer? Little's Law — L = λW
  3. Your endpoint returns 202 — what have you promised the caller, and what can still be lost? durability boundary · at-most-once
  4. Buffered or unbuffered, and what does each do to tail latency under a burst? queueing behavior
  5. Who closes the channel, and why can't it be the senders? ownership
  6. How do you know your store is thread-safe? Name two failures and which tool catches each. data race vs. lost update
  7. What does -race prove, and what does it miss? no false positives, many false negatives
  8. Your store hands back a slice — what's the risk, and why isn't a mutex enough? aliasing · encapsulation
  9. What's the production failure mode of an unbounded queue? OOM · unbounded latency
  10. Why buffer in memory at all when you could write straight to SQS? batching · failure isolation · durability of the 202
  11. You chose fire-and-forget over group commit. What did you trade, what would make you switch, and how would you know your loss rate? at-most-once · end-to-end argument
  12. Queue full returns 503, not 429. Why, and how did you test that you shed? load shedding · overload vs. rate limit
  13. Your framework only offers a global mode switch. Where did you call it, and what does that choice hide? global state · composition root
