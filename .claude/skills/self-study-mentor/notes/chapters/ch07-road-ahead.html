§1M3 · Delivery guarantees

GuaranteeMeansHow you get it
At-most-onceDelivered once or not at all. Never duplicated.Never retry. (Athenas today.)
At-least-onceDelivered one or more times. Never lost (within limits).Retry until acknowledged. Duplicates happen when the ack is lost.
Exactly-onceThe effect happens once.At-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing.

Why exactly-once is a property of the consumer: over an unreliable network, a sender can't know whether a message whose acknowledgement was lost actually arrived (the Two Generals problem). So it has to choose: resend (risk a duplicate) or don't (risk a loss). Real systems choose resend and make the receiver idempotent, so a duplicate has no extra effect. That's often called “effectively once”.

Why consumers must be idempotent: SQS standard queues deliver at least once, and occasionally deliver a message twice even without failures. Idempotency usually comes from a natural key: an upsert on (device ID, timestamp) stores a duplicate reading once. Or keep a table of processed message IDs.

What a visibility timeout buys: when a consumer receives a message, SQS hides it from other consumers for the visibility timeout. If the consumer deletes it in time, it's done. If the consumer crashes, the message reappears and someone else gets it. That's automatic retry after a crash without any locks or coordination between consumers. The cost: the timeout must be longer than processing time, or a slow-but-healthy consumer's message reappears and is processed twice (a consumer can extend it with ChangeMessageVisibility). After too many receives (maxReceiveCount), a redrive policy moves the message to a dead-letter queue so one bad message can't block or loop forever.

FIFO queues add ordering per message group and deduplication within a 5-minute window, at the cost of lower throughput limits. Your Chapter 3 §5 decision (no order through the pipeline, sort on read) means standard queues are fine.

§2M4 · Outbox instead of two-phase commit

The problem: write a row to Postgres and publish an event, so that both happen or neither does. Writing then publishing can crash in between (row, no event). Publishing then writing can crash too (event, no row).

Two-phase commit (2PC): a coordinator asks every participant to prepare (promise it can commit), and only if all agree tells them to commit. Why not:

  • Every participant must support it (XA). Message brokers like SQS and Kafka don't take part as XA resources.
  • It's blocking: if the coordinator fails after participants prepare, they hold locks and wait, unable to decide on their own.
  • Latency and availability: every transaction waits on the slowest participant, and any participant being down stops all writes.

The transactional outbox: in one local transaction, write the telemetry row and an outbox row describing the event. Postgres guarantees both or neither. A separate relay polls the outbox (or reads the database's change log, as Debezium does), publishes each event, and marks it sent.

What it costs you in exchange for atomicity:

  • At-least-once publishing: the relay can publish and crash before marking the row sent, so it publishes again. Consumers must be idempotent (§1).
  • Publish latency: up to one poll interval.
  • Database load: an extra write per event, polling queries, and a table that needs cleanup.
  • Ordering across relays needs care.

It also closes Chapter 3 §6's crash window: the 202 can go out after a durable write. The outbox is a durable queue drained by workers, which is Chapter 3 again.

§3M5 · Partitions, lag, rebalancing

A Kafka topic is split into partitions, each an ordered, append-only log. A record's key is hashed to pick its partition. Within a consumer group, each partition is read by at most one consumer.

Partition ordering vs. throughput: order is guaranteed only within a partition. More partitions mean more consumers can work in parallel, so more throughput, but no order across partitions. A group's parallelism is capped at its partition count. Keying by device keeps each device's records in order (Chapter 3's Fig 3.4, right side), with the same hot-partition risk if one key is very busy.

Consumer lag is, per partition, the latest offset in the log minus the consumer group's committed offset: how many records are waiting. It's queue depth. Lag that keeps growing means consumers are slower than producers, the M2 question again. Because Kafka keeps the backlog on disk, lag costs freshness rather than data, until retention deletes records nobody has read.

Why rebalancing is the hard part: when a consumer joins, leaves, or is presumed dead, partitions are reassigned. With the classic “eager” protocol, every consumer stops and gives up all its partitions while the group reorganizes, so a deploy that restarts consumers one by one causes repeated pauses. Work that was processed but not yet committed is processed again by the new owner, so duplicates appear. A consumer that takes too long between polls is presumed dead and triggers a rebalance itself. Mitigations exist: cooperative (incremental) rebalancing, static membership, and Kafka's newer consumer group protocol, which moves assignment to the broker.

§4M6 · Linearizable rate-limit state

Linearizable means every operation appears to take effect at a single instant between when it was called and when it returned, and all clients see operations in an order that's consistent with real time. It behaves like one copy of the data, even if there are several.

For a global rate limit across pods, that's what makes the limit exact: if the counter is at 99 of 100, two pods can't both see 99 and both admit a request. Redis executes commands one at a time, so an INCR with an expiry, or a Lua script for a multi-step token bucket, is atomic on a single primary. (Caveat for an audit: Redis replication is asynchronous, so a failover can lose recent increments. It's linearizable on one node, not across failover.)

Why coordination is expensive:

  • Latency: every request now waits on a network round trip to Redis.
  • Availability: if Redis is unreachable, you have to choose to fail open (no limits) or fail closed (refuse traffic).
  • Contention: a hot key for a busy client serializes on one Redis node.

The alternative is to coordinate less: each pod enforces a local share of the limit and syncs occasionally. That's cheaper and more available, but approximate. It's the same consistency-for-cost trade that runs through the whole curriculum. This is also where the 429 from Chapter 3 §4 finally arrives.

§5M7 · The four keys

MetricMeasuresKind
Deployment frequencyHow often you deploy to production.Throughput
Lead time for changesTime from commit to running in production.Throughput
Change failure rateFraction of deployments that cause a failure needing remediation.Stability
Time to restore serviceHow long it takes to recover from a failed deployment or incident.Stability

(Recent DORA reports rename the last one “failed deployment recovery time” and add a fifth, rework rate. Know the four; mention the update.)

What counts as a successful deploy is a definition you have to choose and write down, or the numbers are meaningless. A reasonable one: the change reached production and didn't need a rollback, hotfix, or fix-forward within some window. A pipeline that went green isn't enough on its own.

Which two need incident data: change failure rate and time to restore. Deployment frequency and lead time come straight from git and the CI/CD system. The stability metrics need to know which deploys caused failures and when service came back, which means linking incidents to deploys. That's the part most teams can't automate, and the reason M7 is about observability as much as pipelines.
