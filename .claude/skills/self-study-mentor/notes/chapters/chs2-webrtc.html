Chapter S2 · Stretch: video streaming

Video II: WebRTC and Real-Time Transport

WebRTC gets video from a camera to a browser in a few hundred milliseconds by choosing loss over delay at every layer. This chapter walks through session setup, NAT traversal, how lost packets are repaired or ignored, how the sender reacts to a bad network, and where the latency goes.

What the standard leaves to you: the signaling transport and protocol, running STUN and TURN servers, authentication and authorization (who is allowed to watch which camera), and everything about rooms or sessions.

§2NAT, STUN, and TURN

Most devices sit behind NAT, so neither peer knows the address the other should send to. STUN is a tiny service that tells a client what its public address and port look like from outside. That's enough to punch through most home NATs. It fails with symmetric NAT (which picks a different public port per destination), with carrier-grade NAT on many mobile networks, and with firewalls that block UDP. Then TURN is the fallback: a server that relays every packet between the peers.

What fraction needs TURN? Commonly cited figures range from under 10% to around 20% of sessions, and the real number depends heavily on your users' networks; corporate and cellular networks push it up. Don't quote a number in an interview without saying how you'd measure yours: read the selected candidate-pair from getStats() and check whether the local or remote candidate type is relay. Count the fraction over all sessions.

What TURN costs:

  • Bandwidth: every relayed byte passes through your server twice (in and out), and you pay the egress.
  • Latency: an extra hop, worse if the TURN server is far from both peers. Run them in several regions.
  • Capacity: TURN servers must be sized for peak relayed bitrate, not session count.
  • The last resort: TURN over TCP or TLS on port 443 gets through restrictive firewalls, but brings TCP's problems with it (§3).

§3UDP over TCP

TCP delivers every byte, in order. If one packet is lost, everything after it waits until the retransmission arrives: head-of-line blocking. For a file that's correct. For live video it's backwards: a frame that arrives 300 ms late is useless, and holding newer frames behind it makes everything late.

So WebRTC uses UDP and decides per packet whether a loss is worth repairing (§5). It chooses loss over delay: a glitch now is better than a picture that drifts further behind real time.

When it falls back to TCP: when UDP is blocked entirely, ICE can use TURN over TCP or TLS. Quality suffers: retransmissions cause latency spikes and stalls, and WebRTC's congestion control and TCP's own congestion control both react to the same loss, so the stream backs off too far and recovers slowly.

§4RTP, RTCP, and the jitter buffer

RTP carries media. Each packet has a sequence number (to detect loss and reorder), a timestamp (when to play it), and an SSRC, a random identifier for the stream. Audio and video are separate RTP streams with separate SSRCs.

RTCP carries control and feedback alongside: receiver reports (loss fraction, jitter), sender reports (for audio/video sync and round-trip time), NACKs, keyframe requests, and congestion-control feedback. Most of the numbers in getStats() come from RTCP.

The jitter buffer on the receiver holds packets briefly so it can reorder them, wait for retransmissions, and smooth out uneven arrival times before handing frames to the decoder. It's adaptive: on a jittery network it grows (more latency, fewer glitches); on a clean one it shrinks. It's the main latency control a receiver has.

§5NACK, FEC, and PLI

ToolWhat it doesWhen it's the wrong tool
NACKThe receiver reports specific lost sequence numbers; the sender retransmits them.When the round trip is long compared with the jitter buffer: the retransmission arrives after the frame was due. Also when loss comes from congestion, since retransmissions add load to a full link.
FEC (forward error correction)The sender adds redundant data up front, so the receiver can rebuild some lost packets without asking. (ULPFEC, FlexFEC for video; Opus has in-band FEC for audio.)It costs bandwidth all the time, even with no loss. Wrong on a congested link (it adds load), and useless against bursts longer than it can cover.
PLI / FIR (picture loss indication / full intra request)The receiver asks for a new keyframe because it can't decode what it has.Keyframes are large. Frequent requests cause keyframe storms that make congestion worse. With many viewers behind an SFU, every viewer's loss can trigger a request to the camera, so the SFU should combine and rate-limit them.

The order of escalation: retransmit if there's time (NACK); otherwise ride it out with redundancy (FEC); if the decoder's references are broken, reset with a keyframe (PLI/FIR).

§6Congestion control

A home camera's uplink is usually the narrowest link. WebRTC senders estimate how much bandwidth is available and change the encoder's target bitrate to match. Google Congestion Control (GCC), used by browsers, combines two signals:

  • Delay-based: if packets start arriving with growing gaps compared with how they were sent, a queue is building somewhere on the path. Back off before loss starts. The receiver reports arrival times using transport-wide congestion control (TWCC) feedback.
  • Loss-based: sustained loss above a threshold also lowers the estimate.

The encoder responds by lowering bitrate, then resolution or frame rate. This is backpressure in the sense of Chapter 5 §6: the sender slows down to match what the network can carry, and the queue it's avoiding is in the home router (bufferbloat).

What you'd see in getStats() on a bad uplink:

  • candidate-pair.availableOutgoingBitrate falling.
  • outbound-rtp.qualityLimitationReason becoming "bandwidth", with frameWidth, frameHeight, or framesPerSecond dropping.
  • remote-inbound-rtp.roundTripTime rising (queues growing) and fractionLost rising.
  • nackCount and retransmitted packets climbing.

§7Diagnosing a frozen picture

The viewer's video freezes but audio keeps playing. Audio and video are separate RTP streams, so the network path is up. The problem is specific to video. Likely causes, and the receiver's inbound-rtp (video) stats that tell them apart:

CauseWhat the stats show
Video packets stopped arriving (sender paused video, or congestion control dropped it to protect audio)packetsReceived and bytesReceived flat.
Lost reference or keyframe; decoder waiting for a new keyframePackets still arriving, framesDecoded flat, pliCount or firCount rising. keyFramesDecoded doesn't increase until one arrives. If PLIs keep rising with no keyframe, the sender (or SFU) isn't honouring them.
Decoder stall or failure (for example a hardware decoder error)Packets arriving, keyframes arriving, framesDecoded still flat; or frames decoded but not rendered (framesDropped rising).
Severe jitter or lossfreezeCount and totalFreezesDuration rising, packetsLost and jitter high, jitter buffer delay growing.

The method is the part to say out loud: follow the frame through the pipeline (received → decoded → rendered) and find the first counter that stops moving.

For a home camera: P2P is attractive for one viewer on the home network. As soon as two family members watch at once, P2P doubles the camera's uplink, so the product wants an SFU or media server to fan out. That's also where recording can tap the stream. (Pion's broadcast example is a tiny SFU.)

§9Where glass-to-glass latency goes

Glass-to-glass latency is the time from light hitting the camera's sensor to the picture appearing on the viewer's screen. Stages, with rough figures for WebRTC:

StageTypicalNotes
CaptureUp to one frame interval (33 ms at 30 fps)Plus sensor exposure.
EncodeA few ms to a frameLookahead and B-frames add more, which is why real-time encoders avoid them.
Packetize and paceA few msThe pacer spreads a large keyframe over time to avoid bursts.
NetworkOne-way delay, plus queueingBufferbloat in the home router can add hundreds of milliseconds.
Server hop (SFU/TURN)A few ms, plus distance
Jitter bufferTens to hundreds of msAdaptive; usually the largest adjustable piece.
Decode and renderA frame or twoIncluding waiting for the display's refresh.

Which to attack first: the one you've measured as largest, which in practice is usually buffering: the jitter buffer for WebRTC, or for HLS the segment duration times the number of segments the player buffers (Chapter S3 §1), which dwarfs everything else. After that, network queueing.
