Video I: Codecs, GOPs, and Containers

Before any protocol matters, video has to get roughly two hundred times smaller. This chapter covers how a codec does that, why keyframes drive nearly every operational trade-off, the difference between a codec and a container, and when a server has to transcode.

§1Why compression is the whole game

Raw 1080p at 30 frames per second, 8-bit with 4:2:0 chroma subsampling (1.5 bytes per pixel): 1920 × 1080 × 1.5 × 30 ≈ 93 MB/s, or about 750 Mbit/s. A home uplink might be 10–20 Mbit/s. A good H.264 encode of the same video is around 2–6 Mbit/s, so the codec is doing roughly a 150–300× reduction.

It gets there with two kinds of prediction: intra (predict a block from its neighbours in the same frame) and inter (predict a block from a similar block in another frame, plus a motion vector). Then it transforms the leftover error, quantizes it (the lossy step, where bitrate is traded for quality), and entropy-codes the result.

§2Frame types and the GOP

FramePredicted fromSizeNotes
I (intra, keyframe)Nothing outside itselfLargest, often several times a P-frameDecodable on its own. In H.264, an IDR frame is an I-frame that also tells the decoder to discard earlier references, so decoding can start cleanly there.
P (predicted)Earlier framesSmallDepends on its references. If one is lost, errors carry forward.
B (bi-predicted)Earlier and later framesSmallestNeeds a future frame first, so frames are encoded out of display order. That reordering adds latency, which is why real-time WebRTC encoders generally don't use B-frames.

A GOP (group of pictures) runs from one keyframe to the next. The keyframe interval (GOP length) is usually given in frames or seconds: a 2-second GOP at 30 fps is 60 frames.

§3What keyframe interval decides

ConcernShort GOP (frequent keyframes)Long GOP (rare keyframes)
Join timeFast. A new viewer can only start decoding at a keyframe, so it waits up to one GOP.Slow. A viewer may stare at a blank player for seconds.
SeekPrecise. Playback can only start cleanly at a keyframe.Coarse, or the player must decode from an earlier keyframe.
BandwidthHigher. Keyframes are expensive.Lower for the same quality.
Recovery after lossFast. A corrupted reference only lasts until the next keyframe.Slow, unless the receiver asks for a keyframe (PLI/FIR, Chapter 8 §5).
Segmenting (HLS)Segments must start on a keyframe, so the GOP has to divide the segment length.Forces long segments, which means more latency.

WebRTC usually runs very long or open-ended GOPs and sends a keyframe on request when a viewer joins or loses packets. HLS needs a fixed, regular keyframe interval aligned to segment boundaries. A camera serving both has to satisfy both.

§4Codec vs. container

A codec defines the compressed bitstream: H.264, HEVC, VP8, VP9, AV1 for video; AAC, Opus for audio. A container wraps one or more of those streams with timestamps, track information, and often an index. The same H.264 stream can sit in different containers, and changing the container doesn't touch the compressed video.

ContainerShapeWhere you'll meet it
MPEG-TSA stream of fixed 188-byte packets, designed for broadcast. Survives truncation and joins mid-stream.Classic HLS segments (.ts).
Fragmented MP4 (fMP4 / CMAF)An init segment, then self-contained fragments.Modern HLS and DASH, and Low-Latency HLS.
MKV (Matroska)Flexible, block-based, streamable.Kinesis Video Streams stores media as MKV fragments.
IVFA minimal header plus length-prefixed frames.VP8/VP9 frames on disk; Pion's save-to-disk writes VP8 to IVF (and Opus to Ogg).
Plain MP4Its index (the moov box) is often written at the end of the file.Downloads and clips. Dangerous for recording, below.

Why the container matters for recording: a recorder can crash. A plain MP4 whose index would have been written at the end is unplayable if the writer dies first, so a 10-minute recording becomes zero minutes. Streaming containers (MPEG-TS, fMP4, MKV) are written as independent pieces, so a crash only loses the last piece. That's the same at-most-once thinking as Chapter 1, applied to files.

§5Transcode vs. transmux

Transmux (ffmpeg -c copy)Transcode (for example -c:v libx264)
What happensUnwrap from one container, rewrap in another. The compressed frames are unchanged.Decode to raw frames, then encode again.
CPU per streamClose to zero. It's I/O.A real share of a core per stream, or a GPU.
Latency addedNegligible.Encoder buffering, often hundreds of milliseconds or more.
QualityIdentical.Generation loss: each lossy encode degrades it a little.
What it enablesChanging protocol or packaging.Changing codec, resolution, or bitrate; building an adaptive-bitrate ladder.

Designing a fleet so the server rarely transcodes

  • Encode once, in a codec every viewer can play. Have cameras produce H.264 at a profile browsers and HLS players accept. Then live (WebRTC) and recorded (HLS) are both transmux operations.
  • Negotiate the codec, don't convert it. In WebRTC, offer H.264 in SDP so the browser doesn't pick VP8 (the spike's decision).
  • Get multiple qualities from the source. Simulcast (the camera sends two or three encodings) or scalable coding lets a server pick a layer per viewer, instead of transcoding to make one.
  • Transcode only on demand, for the rare viewer or clip that needs it, and cache the result.

This is the cost structure a media server is built around: transmuxing is cheap enough to do for every stream; transcoding is expensive enough to plan capacity for.

§6Choosing a codec for a battery camera

A battery camera can't afford software encoding, so the deciding question is usually what the camera's chip can encode in hardware. Then browser support, then licensing. The compression figures below are rough, commonly cited numbers; they vary a lot by content and encoder.

CodecCompressionHardware encode on camera chipsBrowser playbackLicensing
H.264 / AVCBaselineUniversalUniversal; mandatory in WebRTCRoyalty-bearing, with mature, well-understood patent pools
HEVC / H.265Roughly 25–50% smaller than H.264 for the same qualityCommon on newer chipsUneven: Safari yes; others depend on platform and hardwareRoyalty-bearing, split across several pools, historically messy
VP8Around H.264 or a bit worseRareUniversal in WebRTC; also mandatory thereRoyalty-free
VP9Similar to HEVCLess common on low-cost chipsGood in Chrome/Firefox; Safari partialRoyalty-free
AV1Best of the groupOnly on recent chipsGrowing quicklyRoyalty-free (AOMedia)

For a fleet today, H.264 is the default answer because it's the only codec that's both hardware-encoded everywhere and playable everywhere, including in HLS and WebRTC. HEVC or AV1 save bandwidth and storage (which matters at fleet scale, Chapter 9 §8), but only on hardware that supports them, and they may force transcoding for viewers who can't decode them. That turns a bandwidth saving into a compute cost.
