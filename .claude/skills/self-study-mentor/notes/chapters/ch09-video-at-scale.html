Chapter 9 · Video track, week 3

Video III: Delivery, Media Servers, and Scale

Live view and recorded playback want opposite things: one wants the lowest possible delay, the other wants durable, seekable storage that caches well. This chapter covers how a camera product serves both, what a media server and Kinesis Video Streams take off your hands, and the capacity math for a fleet of a million cameras.

§1HLS, and why it caches

HTTP Live Streaming cuts video into segments (often 2–6 seconds each) and lists them in a playlist (.m3u8). The player fetches the playlist, then the segments, over plain HTTP. For live streams it re-fetches the playlist to discover new segments.

Latency: a segment can't be published until it's complete, and players typically buffer about three segments before starting, for smooth adaptive-bitrate switching. With 6-second segments that's roughly 18 seconds behind live, plus encoding and packaging. Typical HLS latency is somewhere around 10–30 seconds, or about 6 with short segments and tuning.

§2Low-Latency HLS

LL-HLS (Apple's extension) gets latency to roughly 2–5 seconds without giving up HTTP and CDNs. Its main tools:

  • Partial segments: each segment is published in small parts (a few hundred milliseconds each) as they're encoded, so players don't wait for a whole segment.
  • Blocking playlist reload: the player asks for the playlist with the next part number, and the server holds the request until that part exists. No polling delay.
  • Preload hints (request the next part before it exists) and delta playlists (send only what changed).

What it trades: many more HTTP requests per viewer, more load on the CDN and the origin, a server that must support blocking requests, and less buffer, so more risk of stalls on a bad network.

Why it isn't WebRTC: it still runs over TCP, so a lost packet still stalls (Chapter 8 §3). It still buffers whole parts and keeps a playback buffer for adaptive bitrate. It still has no per-packet loss handling or real-time congestion control. It's built to keep the cacheability of HLS, and that caps how low it can go.

§3Live and recorded in one product

A camera app typically uses WebRTC for live view (you want to see who's at the door now, and maybe talk back) and HLS from storage for playback (Kinesis Video Streams can serve stored fragments as HLS, in live or on-demand mode). Recording wants durable, time-indexed storage, seeking, and scrubbing, and none of that is what WebRTC is for.

Where the switch happens in the UI: usually the moment the user scrubs back on the timeline, or opens an event clip. The player tears down the WebRTC session and starts an HLS session for the selected time.

What the user sees at the boundary: a short loading spinner while the HLS session starts and the first segment loads; possibly a change in quality; and, if they scrub forward to “now” in HLS, a picture that's several seconds behind true live. So the app usually needs a “Go live” control that switches back to WebRTC, rather than letting HLS pretend to be live.

§4What a media server does

A media server such as MistServer takes any protocol in and any protocol out: RTMP, SRT, RTSP, or WHIP in; HLS, DASH, or WebRTC out. It mostly transmuxes (Chapter 7 §5), which is why one server can handle many streams. It also fans one input out to many viewers, records, and can enforce access.

Why put one between the camera and viewers instead of going P2P:

  • The camera uploads once, however many people watch. Uplink and battery stay flat.
  • Protocol translation: the camera speaks one protocol, and each viewer gets whatever suits it.
  • One place to record, authorize, and measure, which you control.
  • NAT traversal gets simpler: each side connects to a server with a public address instead of to each other.

The cost is the server fleet itself: bandwidth, capacity planning, and operations.

§5Kinesis Video Streams, and build vs. buy

What KVS handles for you

  • Ingest: the producer SDK (and the GStreamer kvssink plugin) pushes media as MKV fragments.
  • Durable, time-indexed storage with a retention period you set (DataRetentionInHours; 0 means no storage, pass-through only).
  • Playback: HLS and DASH sessions over stored or live fragments, and clip extraction to MP4.
  • KVS WebRTC (a separate capability): managed signaling channels plus STUN and TURN. With WebRTC ingestion, one session can serve live viewers and record to a stream.

What's left for you to build

  • Device identity, provisioning, and credentials for every camera.
  • Authorization: which user may watch which camera.
  • Event detection, and the metadata index of which events happened when (KVS stores media; it doesn't know a doorbell rang).
  • The app and player, the live/recorded switch (§3), and cost controls.
  • Fan-out beyond KVS WebRTC's per-channel viewer limits. Check the current quotas before designing around them.

Choosing a retention period

Retention is a product, legal, and cost decision at once. Storage cost grows linearly with retention days and with how much you record. Subscription tiers often map to retention (for example 30 vs. 60 days). Privacy cuts the other way: deleting sooner is a feature, and some jurisdictions require it.

Event-triggered recording changes the bill most. Recording only around motion or doorbell events stores minutes per day instead of 24 hours (§8 has the numbers). The catch is the moment before the event: to include it, the camera keeps a few seconds of pre-roll in a ring buffer, the same structure as Chapter 1 §3, and uploads it when the event fires.

What would make you consolidate: one system covering both well enough (for example KVS WebRTC with ingestion serving live and recording together), operations cost of running two systems exceeding the savings, or scale at which owning storage is clearly cheaper. The split itself is defensible: each half is optimized for its job.

§6Stream-quality telemetry

What goes on a dashboard, and what pages

Fleet-level, aggregated signals (Chapter 6's RED and USE, applied to video):

  • Session setup success rate, and time to first frame (p50/p95).
  • TURN usage fraction (Chapter 8 §2).
  • Freeze rate and total freeze time per viewing minute; packet loss, round-trip time, and bitrate distributions.
  • Recording upload lag and failed fragment rate.

Page on: symptoms users feel, sustained against an SLO: setup failure rate and time-to-first-frame burning budget, or recording ingest failing. Everything else goes on the dashboard. Never put device_id or SSRC in a metric label: a million cameras means millions of series, and SSRCs are random per stream, so they churn constantly (Chapter 6 §2). Per-camera detail belongs in logs, traces, or a per-device store like Athenas.

Counters, not rates

getStats() returns mostly cumulative counters (bytesReceived, packetsLost, framesDropped) and a few gauges (jitter, roundTripTime). Two ways to ship them:

  • Ship the raw counters. A lost sample only costs resolution: the next sample still contains everything that happened, and the rate between any two samples can be computed at query time. This is Prometheus's rate() model, which also detects counter resets (a restarted connection starts again at zero).
  • Ship deltas or rates computed on the producer. Each sample carries data no other sample has. Lose it, and that interval is gone.

So counters make Athenas's at-most-once delivery more tolerable, not less. That's worth checking against the reversal condition in the 2026-10-09 decision, which named counters as a reason to reconsider.

§7The camera's own queue

A camera's upload is slower than its encoder produces. This is the M2 question in a video setting: where's the queue, what's the backpressure signal, and what drops first?

  • The queue: the encoder's output buffer, the sender's pacer, and the socket's send buffer. Beyond that, the home router's buffer, where bufferbloat lives.
  • The backpressure signal: the congestion controller's bandwidth estimate falling (Chapter 8 §6), and the pacer queue growing.
  • What drops first, in order: lower the encoder's bitrate; then resolution or frame rate; then drop frames nothing else depends on (non-reference frames or a temporal layer). Keep audio ahead of video. Never drop a keyframe and keep sending the frames that depend on it.
  • For recording, don't drop at all: buffer to local storage (an SD card) and upload later. Live can't wait; recording can.

The parallel with Athenas is exact: a bounded queue, a decision about what to refuse when it's full, and different answers for data that's only useful now (live, telemetry) versus data that must arrive eventually (recording).

Things to say next: averages hide peaks. Events correlate in time (mornings, deliveries, a storm setting off motion sensors across a region), so design ingest for a peak several times the average. The answer is most sensitive to bitrate and to events per day, so codec choice (Chapter 7 §6) and motion-detection quality are both cost levers. And recording dominates storage, while live view dominates server bandwidth.
